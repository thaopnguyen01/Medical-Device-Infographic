What It Is

What is a medical device?

The FD&C Act's definition is broad by design — it covers everything from a personal lubricant to an implanted pacemaker. What determines how strictly a product is regulated isn't what it's made of, but the risk it poses and the claims made about it.

FDA Legal Definition, Condensed

A medical device is any instrument, apparatus, or article — including software — intended to diagnose, treat, or prevent disease, or to affect the body's structure or function, without being metabolized like a drug.

I
Low Risk

Class I

Minimal potential for harm. Most are exempt from premarket review — bandages, exam gloves, handheld surgical tools.

II
Moderate Risk

Class II

Requires demonstrated safety controls. Most need 510(k) clearance — blood pressure cuffs, contact lenses, condoms.

III
High Risk

Class III

Sustains or supports life, or presents significant injury risk. Requires full FDA approval — pacemakers, dermal fillers, breast implants.

Getting to Market

How medical devices get regulated

A device's class determines its path to market — and that path is exactly what a marketplace listing skips when it sells directly to a consumer with no clinical gatekeeper involved.

Class I

Exempt

Most Class I devices skip premarket review entirely. General controls (labeling, manufacturing standards) still apply.

Class II

510(k) Clearance

Manufacturer shows the device is "substantially equivalent" to one already on the market. Clearance, not approval.

Class III

Premarket Approval (PMA)

Full clinical evidence review required before the FDA approves the device for sale. The highest bar in the system.

Monitoring teams track the taxonomy below, not just "Class III" as one bucket — since a listing can be reportable for different reasons.

Device Groups

The groups behind the numbers

Individual product names roll up into a handful of functional groups. Knowing the group tells you what oversight is being skipped when the device shows up unsupervised.

Injectable & Aesthetic Devices

Substances or devices placed under the skin or injected to alter appearance — volume, texture, or contour.

e.g., dermal fillers, RF microneedling devices
Reproductive & Intimate Health

High-volume, everyday-use devices tied to sexual health and menstrual care.

e.g., personal lubricants, condoms, tampons
Body Contouring & Weight Management

Devices marketed to reshape the body non-surgically, most originally designed for supervised clinical settings.

e.g., liposuction devices, cryolipolysis machines
Pain & Rehabilitation

Therapeutic devices intended to treat musculoskeletal or nerve-related pain, usually under clinical guidance.

e.g., shockwave therapy devices, therapeutic lasers
Monitoring & Diagnostic Devices

Devices intended to measure a physiological value or screen for a condition — accuracy is the entire point.

e.g., insulin injector pens, bilirubinometers, hyperbaric chambers
Vision & Optical Devices

Devices worn on or in the eye — corrective, cosmetic, or both.

e.g., contact lenses, including non-corrective decorative lenses
The Risk

Why unauthorized marketplace sales are dangerous

A device isn't risky because of what it's made of — it's risky because of what's missing when it's sold outside its intended pathway.

No clinical oversight for administration

Devices designed to be used by a trained provider — injectables, contouring equipment, therapeutic lasers — carry real procedural risk when self-administered: vascular occlusion and blindness from improperly placed filler, burns from unsupervised RF or cryo devices, and tissue damage from misapplied therapeutic energy.

No manufacturing or quality oversight

An FDA pathway exists to verify sterility, dosing accuracy, and material safety. Devices sold outside that pathway have none of it verified — counterfeit filler, contaminated lubricant, or an insulin pen with an inaccurate dose are all indistinguishable from the authentic product until something goes wrong.

No prescription gate where one is required

Prescription-only devices exist because a clinician needs to rule out contraindications first. Selling directly to a consumer removes that check entirely — the buyer has no way to know if the device is appropriate for them.

No adverse event reporting or recall reach

Authorized devices are traceable — if something goes wrong, the FDA can issue a recall and reach affected users. An unauthorized listing has no such trail: if a batch is defective, buyers have no way to be notified.

Marketing that hides the device status

Listings frequently frame a regulated device as a "beauty tool" or "wellness gadget" rather than a medical device — which is often exactly what lets it bypass a platform's own content moderation in the first place.
