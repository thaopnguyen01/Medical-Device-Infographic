Medical Device Regulation & Marketplace Safety

What medical devices are, why unauthorized sales are risky, and where they tend to show up.

A two-part guide to how medical devices are regulated and why that regulation matters — followed by a look at the categories of devices most often sold outside the channels meant to keep them safe.

What It Is

What is a medical device?

The FD&C Act's definition is broad by design — it covers everything from a personal lubricant to an implanted pacemaker. What determines how strictly a product is regulated isn't what it's made of, but the risk it poses and the claims made about it.

FDA Legal Definition, Condensed

A medical device is any instrument, apparatus, or article — including software — intended to diagnose, treat, or prevent disease, or to affect the body's structure or function, without being metabolized like a drug.

I
Low Risk

Class I

Minimal potential for harm. Most are exempt from premarket review — bandages, exam gloves, handheld surgical tools.

II
Moderate Risk

Class II

Requires demonstrated safety controls. Most need 510(k) clearance — blood pressure cuffs, contact lenses, condoms.

III
High Risk

Class III

Sustains or supports life, or presents significant injury risk. Requires full FDA approval — pacemakers, dermal fillers, breast implants.

Getting to Market

How medical devices get regulated

A device's class determines its path to market — and that path is exactly what a marketplace listing skips when it sells directly to a consumer with no clinical gatekeeper involved.

Class I

Exempt

Most Class I devices skip premarket review entirely. General controls (labeling, manufacturing standards) still apply.

Class II

510(k) Clearance

Manufacturer shows the device is "substantially equivalent" to one already on the market. Clearance, not approval.

Class III

Premarket Approval (PMA)

Full clinical evidence review required before the FDA approves the device for sale. The highest bar in the system.

Class isn't the whole story, either. Some devices — injectable fillers and cosmetic contact lenses among them — carry a prescription requirement on top of their class, because a clinician needs to rule out contraindications or fit the device to the individual before it's used. A device can be fully cleared or approved and still be illegal to sell direct-to-consumer if that prescription gate is skipped.

Device Groups

The groups behind the numbers

Individual product names roll up into a handful of functional groups. Knowing the group tells you what oversight is being skipped when the device shows up unsupervised.

Injectable & Aesthetic Devices

Substances or devices placed under the skin or injected to alter appearance — volume, texture, or contour.

e.g., dermal fillers, RF microneedling devices
Reproductive & Intimate Health

High-volume, everyday-use devices tied to sexual health and menstrual care.

e.g., personal lubricants, condoms, tampons
Body Contouring & Weight Management

Devices marketed to reshape the body non-surgically, most originally designed for supervised clinical settings.

e.g., liposuction devices, cryolipolysis machines
Pain & Rehabilitation

Therapeutic devices intended to treat musculoskeletal or nerve-related pain, usually under clinical guidance.

e.g., shockwave therapy devices, therapeutic lasers
Monitoring & Diagnostic Devices

Devices intended to measure a physiological value or screen for a condition — accuracy is the entire point.

e.g., insulin injector pens, bilirubinometers, hyperbaric chambers
Vision & Optical Devices

Devices worn on or in the eye — corrective, cosmetic, or both.

e.g., contact lenses, including non-corrective decorative lenses
The Risk

Why unauthorized marketplace sales are dangerous

A device isn't risky because of what it's made of — it's risky because of what's missing when it's sold outside its intended pathway.

No clinical oversight for administration

Devices designed to be used by a trained provider — injectables, contouring equipment, therapeutic lasers — carry real procedural risk when self-administered: vascular occlusion and blindness from improperly placed filler, burns from unsupervised RF or cryo devices, and tissue damage from misapplied therapeutic energy.

No manufacturing or quality oversight

An FDA pathway exists to verify sterility, dosing accuracy, and material safety. Devices sold outside that pathway have none of it verified — counterfeit filler, contaminated lubricant, or an insulin pen with an inaccurate dose are all indistinguishable from the authentic product until something goes wrong.

No prescription gate where one is required

Prescription-only devices exist because a clinician needs to rule out contraindications first. Selling directly to a consumer removes that check entirely — the buyer has no way to know if the device is appropriate for them.

No adverse event reporting or recall reach

Authorized devices are traceable — if something goes wrong, the FDA can issue a recall and reach affected users. An unauthorized listing has no such trail: if a batch is defective, buyers have no way to be notified.

Marketing that hides the device status

Listings frequently frame a regulated device as a "beauty tool" or "wellness gadget" rather than a medical device — which is often exactly what lets it bypass a platform's own content moderation in the first place.

Product Guide

What each device is, and why a version of it ends up on a marketplace

Legitimate medical use, paired with the reason a consumer-facing version turns up for sale — usually cost, convenience, or a workaround for a professional or prescription requirement.

Reproductive & Intimate Health

Personal Lubricant
510(k)-Required
What it is
Water- or silicone-based product intended to reduce friction during intimate activity; regulated as a device because of the direct-contact and compatibility claims involved (e.g., condom-safe formulations).
Why it's on marketplaces
Extremely high consumer demand, low unit cost, and buyers rarely realize the category is FDA-regulated at all — making it an easy, low-scrutiny import.
Male Condom
510(k)-Required
What it is
Barrier device for contraception and STI prevention; clearance verifies material integrity and burst-strength testing.
Why it's on marketplaces
Bulk, unbranded imports undercut retail pricing significantly — but skip the burst/leak testing that clearance exists to verify.
Tampons
510(k)-Required
What it is
Menstrual hygiene device; regulated for absorbency labeling accuracy and material safety, tied to toxic shock syndrome (TSS) risk.
Why it's on marketplaces
A category that can appear at high velocity in a short window — likely a low-cost import entering a category buyers don't expect to be regulated.

Vision & Optical

Contact Lenses
Class III or Prescription-Only
What it is
Includes both corrective and purely cosmetic (decorative/costume) lenses — both require a fitting and prescription, even lenses with zero corrective power.
Why it's on marketplaces
Decorative color-change lenses are marketed as costume or fashion accessories, letting sellers imply they're not medical at all — despite carrying real corneal infection and vision-damage risk from poor fit or non-sterile manufacturing.

Body Contouring & Weight Management

Liposuction Device
Class III or Prescription-Only
What it is
Surgical equipment for removing fat deposits; intended strictly for use by trained medical professionals in a clinical setting.
Why it's on marketplaces
Sold to non-professionals running unlicensed "med-spa" style cosmetic services — carries burn, perforation, and infection risk with no surgical training behind it.
Cryo Freezing for Weight Loss Machine
Class III or Prescription-Only
What it is
Cryolipolysis ("fat freezing") equipment intended to reduce localized fat under supervised, controlled temperature application.
Why it's on marketplaces
Marketed as a home wellness or "med-spa business starter" device; unsupervised use carries frostbite risk and, paradoxically, can worsen fat deposits if misapplied.

Injectable & Aesthetic Devices

Dermal Filler
Class III or Prescription-Only
What it is
Injectable gel used to add volume or smooth wrinkles, typically hyaluronic-acid based, meant to be placed by a trained provider who knows facial vascular anatomy.
Why it's on marketplaces
Sold direct-to-consumer for self- or unlicensed injection to undercut med-spa prices; misplacement near a blood vessel can cause vascular occlusion, tissue death, or vision loss — none of that oversight exists in a marketplace sale.
RF Microneedling Device
Class III or Prescription-Only
What it is
Combines radiofrequency energy with microneedling to stimulate collagen production for skin tightening or scar treatment — a professional in-office procedure.
Why it's on marketplaces
Home-use versions are marketed as an at-home alternative to med-spa treatments; improper needle depth or unsterile use risks burns, scarring, and infection.

Pain & Rehabilitation

Shockwave Therapy Device
Class III or Prescription-Only
What it is
Delivers acoustic pulses to treat musculoskeletal pain and, in clinical settings, certain forms of erectile dysfunction.
Why it's on marketplaces
Sold direct-to-consumer for both pain relief and ED marketing claims, bypassing the clinical assessment that would normally rule out an underlying condition first.

Monitoring & Diagnostic Devices

Hyperbaric Oxygen Chamber
Class III or Prescription-Only
What it is
Delivers pressurized oxygen for approved indications like wound healing and decompression sickness — requires trained supervision due to fire and pressure-injury risk.
Why it's on marketplaces
Wellness and biohacking marketing sells home units for unapproved uses (anti-aging, cognitive claims), with none of the supervision the approved use case requires.
Insulin Injector Pen
Class III or Prescription-Only
What it is
Delivers precise, prescribed insulin doses for diabetes management — dosing accuracy is the entire safety case for the device.
Why it's on marketplaces
Sold as a cheaper alternative to a prescribed pen; an unauthorized unit has no verified dosing accuracy and no recall trail if a batch is defective — a meaningful risk for a device managing a life-threatening condition.
Transcutaneous Bilirubinometer
Class III or Prescription-Only
What it is
Non-invasive device that screens newborns for jaundice by measuring bilirubin through the skin.
Why it's on marketplaces
Marketed to parents and small clinics for at-home or low-cost screening; an uncalibrated unit risks a false-negative reading that delays treatment for a condition that can cause serious harm if missed.

Dental Devices

Bruxism Mouthguard
510(k)-Required
What it is
Worn overnight to reduce damage from teeth grinding; effective versions are custom-fitted to the wearer's bite.
Why it's on marketplaces
Generic "one-size" versions make therapeutic claims without any dental fitting — a mismatch can worsen bite alignment over time rather than protect it.
Ortho-Bonding
Class III or Prescription-Only
What it is
Adhesive material used by orthodontists to attach brackets to teeth as part of professionally supervised treatment.
Why it's on marketplaces
Sold as part of DIY orthodontic kits aimed at consumers wanting a cheaper alternative to braces — without professional monitoring, misapplied bonding risks enamel damage and poor bite alignment.
